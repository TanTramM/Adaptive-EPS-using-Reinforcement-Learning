1. Introduction

Power steering reduces the effort required to turn a vehicle while retaining steering torque through which the driver perceives the steering response. Whereas hydraulic power steering supplies
assistance through pressurized fluid, electric power steering (EPS) uses an electronically controlled motor, offering energy efficiency and flexibility in tuning the steering characteristics [1].
In column-type EPS (C-EPS), the motor applies assistance to the steering column, which remains mechanically connected to the road wheels. The control objective therefore extends beyond making the
steering wheel easy to turn: the assistance must also produce an appropriate steering effort as the driving conditions change.

A conventional EPS controller determines the basic motor assistance from a nonlinear map of measured torsion-bar torque and vehicle speed. A stabilizing compensator is used to accommodate the
high assist gain and the nonlinear map characteristics [1]. Assist-map design can be organized around a desired relationship between steering effort and vehicle response, combined with the
steady-state inverse characteristics of the chassis and steering mechanism [2]. This approach gives engineers a way to calculate and calibrate assistance for specified operating conditions.
However, a map calibrated for one road condition does not necessarily reproduce the desired steering effort when the road reaction changes [3].

This mismatch is particularly relevant on low-friction roads. Changes in tire lateral force and self-aligning moment can reduce the reaction torque transmitted to the steering mechanism, allowing
the steering effort to become lower than intended [3,4]. The conventional map responds to the changed torque-sensor signal, but it does not explicitly regulate the error relative to a desired
steering torque. Thus, the existence of torque feedback within the map does not by itself guarantee the desired effort. The resulting deviation motivates an explicit torque-tracking objective,
while sensor noise and motor limitations require that any reduction in tracking error be assessed together with the additional torque fluctuations it produces.

Several studies compensate for road-dependent steering effort by modifying the assist characteristic. Li and Xia [5] used road identification based on a neural network to select assistance
characteristics. Fan et al. [4] added a fuzzy compensation current determined from estimated tire-road friction and vehicle speed. Li et al. [6] developed adhesion-dependent assist coefficients
and compensation currents, while a subsequent multi-map strategy incorporated both road adhesion and front-axle load [3]. These approaches demonstrate the importance of accounting for road
conditions, but require friction information, and in the multi-map case axle-load information, in the assistance calculation. Their implementation therefore includes a means of supplying or
estimating those quantities.

Other approaches modify the controller or regulate steering torque directly. Zhao et al. [7] dynamically corrected the steering-characteristic curve and used proportional-integral-derivative
(PID) control to track the motor-assistance command. Lee et al. [8] instead tracked a target steering-wheel torque with adaptive sliding-mode control. Their target was obtained from vehicle-test
data as a function of steering angle, steering angular velocity, and vehicle speed, and robustness was evaluated by varying the nominal rack force from 50% to 150%.

Lee et al. [9] subsequently separated steering-feel generation from steering-torque feedback. They generated a target torque from estimated rack force, designed a linear feedback controller for
tracking, noise attenuation, and robust stability, and used a disturbance observer to suppress rack-force and driver-torque disturbances. Their simulations directly compared target-torque
feedback with a conventional assist map under matched high-frequency attenuation, and parking tests on a production vehicle compared PID, lead-lag, and linear-quadratic-Gaussian feedback
controllers. Therefore, target-torque feedback, explicit noise attenuation, and a direct comparison with a conventional map are established results rather than contributions of the present study.
Related work has also addressed nonlinear EPS stabilization with motor-current constraints [10] and neural-fuzzy motor control under disturbance torque [11].

The scope left open by these studies is narrower. Lee et al. [9] represented driving disturbance with a linear column-angle relation, generated the target from a model-based rack-force estimate,
and validated the controllers in parking maneuvers; their study did not simulate a friction transition with nonlinear tire saturation. It also did not evaluate a controller in which the
conventional map and target-torque feedback act in parallel. Desired-effort data indexed by vehicle speed and lateral acceleration are available in the literature [5]. Using measured lateral
acceleration to select this reference relates the target to the vehicle's actual lateral response without estimating either friction coefficient or rack force. This reference remains condition
dependent and does not prescribe constant steering effort when friction changes.

The question considered here is therefore how a conventional assist map, direct torque feedback, and their parallel combination compare against this common reference when tire-road friction
changes. The specific issues are how much of the reaction-torque change the map can attenuate, whether torque-error feedback improves tracking under the same actuator and sensor constraints, and
whether retaining the map alongside the feedback changes the trade-off between tracking error and high-frequency assist torque.

This study investigates these questions in a nonlinear C-EPS-vehicle simulation. Three controller structures are considered: an assist map with lead-lag compensation (Map), direct PID torque
feedback, and a parallel combination of the map and PID torque-error feedback (MapPID). They are evaluated against the same torque reference with common motor dynamics, assistance limits, and
sensor characteristics. Calibration is separated from the evaluation maneuvers, and the comparison includes both torque tracking and undesirable assist behavior.

The contributions of this study are as follows:
- A local steady-state analysis extends the known structural limitations of an assist map by quantifying its sensitivity to a change in road reaction and by contrasting that sensitivity with
  integral torque-error feedback.
- A common torque-regulation problem applies a published speed-and-lateral-acceleration reference to Map, PID, and their parallel combination, MapPID, without estimating the friction coefficient
  or rack force.
- A comparative simulation assessment uses a nonlinear tire-vehicle model, friction transitions, common motor and sensor constraints, calibration Pareto fronts, and separate evaluation maneuvers
  to quantify tracking, high-frequency assist torque, and reverse assistance. It tests whether retaining the map provides a measurable benefit alongside torque-error feedback.
