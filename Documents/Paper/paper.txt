Friction-Independent Torque Tracking for Column Electric Power Steering: Closing the Over-Assist Gap of Assist Maps with a Lateral-Acceleration Reference
==============================================================================================================================================================================================
[Authors] Tran Minh Tan, Phung Thanh Huy. [Affiliation] Ho Chi Minh City University of Technology (HCMUT), VNU-HCM. WORKING DRAFT: sections are added in order; text in square brackets is open and must be filled or checked before submission.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Abstract
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Electric power steering (EPS) assist maps are calibrated on dry asphalt and see only the driver torque and the vehicle speed. When the tire-road friction coefficient drops during a turn, the torque returned by the road falls, the map keeps assisting as before, and the steering wheel becomes lighter than the driver expects: over-assist. Published remedies estimate the road or the axle load and switch between maps. We ask whether such an estimate is needed at all. We define the desired steering torque from the measured speed and lateral acceleration, which already carry the friction information, so that over-assist becomes the tracking error of the measured column torque with respect to this reference. A first-order analysis shows that an assist map attenuates the friction-induced torque error only by the factor $1/(1+s)$, where $s$ is its local slope, and $s$ is limited by stability and sensor noise, whereas a controller with integral action removes the error in steady state. On a four-state nonlinear column-vehicle model with a Magic-Formula tire, quantized and noisy sensors and a bounded, lagged motor, we compare an assist map with lead(-lag) compensation against PI and PID torque feedback under a protocol fixed before the runs: pass/fail gates, a Pareto comparison of tracking error against command noise, and scoring on dry-road, friction-change and continuous-steering cases, with a report-only case above 0.3 g. [R1: tracking error after a friction drop, map versus PID, in percent of the reference.] [R2: price paid in command noise.] The study is simulation only.
Index Terms: electric power steering, over-assist, road friction, torque tracking, assist map, PID control

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Introduction
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Electric power steering (EPS) has replaced hydraulic power steering in most passenger cars because it saves fuel, is compact, and lets the steering feel be tuned in software [1]. In a column-type EPS (C-EPS) a motor mounted on the steering column adds an assist torque to the torque of the driver, and a torque sensor on the column measures the torque that the driver feels. The core of the controller is the assist map, a nonlinear one-to-one function from the measured driver torque and the vehicle speed to the assist torque; its shape defines the steering feel [1]. Because the gain of the map is high and the map is nonlinear, the loop can vibrate or diverge, so a map is always combined with a stabilizing compensator such as a lead-lag filter [1].
An assist map is calibrated on a dry road. The torque that the road returns to the steering wheel, the self-aligning torque, depends on the tire-road friction coefficient: when the coefficient drops, the lateral force of the front tire saturates earlier and at a lower level, and the lever arm of that force (the pneumatic trail) shrinks, so the returned torque falls. The map does not know this and keeps assisting by the dry-road rule. The wheel becomes lighter than the driver expects at the same speed and lateral acceleration. This is over-assist. It removes the road feel exactly when the grip margin is small, and it has been reported to damage driving safety [2].
The remedies in the literature make the assist road-aware. Li et al. [2] design several maps for different front-axle loads and adhesion coefficients, use a back-propagation (BP) neural network to determine the steering resistance torque, and select the map accordingly. Li and Xia [3] identify the road with a BP neural network and select the assist curve of the identified road. Both approaches depend on an estimate of a quantity that is not measured on the car, and the selected map is then used in an open-loop way. A second line of work replaces the map by intelligent controllers: an adaptive neural-fuzzy inference system with field-oriented motor control is tested against random disturbance torque in [4], and a fuzzy controller with limited motor current is designed in [5]; reviews expect more learning-based, adaptive torque management in EPS [6]. None of these works, to our reading, defines over-assist as a tracking error with respect to a reference that is built only from signals measured on the car [to be verified by a systematic literature search before submission].
The ideal steering-wheel torque is itself a function of measured signals. Experimental and simulation data for several cars give the torque that drivers prefer as a function of the vehicle speed and the lateral acceleration, and the shape of this characteristic is nearly the same for cars of similar size and mass [3], [7]. Lateral acceleration is measured on every car with electronic stability control, and it reflects what the tires can actually deliver on the present road. If the desired torque is defined as $T_{d,ref}(v, a_y)$, a lighter wheel on a slippery road is not an error by itself, because the lateral acceleration is also lower; only the part of the lightness that the lateral acceleration does not explain is over-assist. Over-assist becomes the tracking error $e_T = T_s - T_{d,ref}$ of the sensed torque $T_s$, and feedback of the measured torque can act on it without any estimate of the friction coefficient.
This reformulation raises two questions that the paper answers. First, does a map, even with a stabilizing compensator, fall short of feedback on this error, and why? A first-order analysis in Section 3 shows that in steady state a map attenuates the friction-induced torque error only by the factor $1/(1+s)$, where $s$ is its local slope, which stability and sensor noise limit, while a controller with integral action removes the error. Second, what does feedback cost? Torque feedback raises the gain from sensor noise to the assist command, and the price must be measured against the benefit on an equal footing for every controller.
The contributions are:
C1. A formulation of over-assist as a torque-tracking problem with a reference built only from measured signals and an assist limit derived from the model, and a first-order analysis of why an assist map cannot remove the friction-induced error while an integral controller can.
C2. A comparison protocol fixed before the runs: pass/fail gates (stability margin, steady accuracy), with reverse assist and bang-bang reported, a Pareto comparison of tracking error against command noise on one calibration case, and scoring on single-feature test cases (dry-road accuracy, friction change while cornering, continuous steering) with identical inputs and identical sensor noise for every controller, plus two report-only cases: a speed change while cornering and the extreme region above 0.3 g.
C3. A quantitative comparison of an assist map with lead(-lag) compensation and of PI and PID torque feedback, with a mechanism given for each difference found [summary of the results of Section 6 to be written when the final runs are done].
The study is limited to simulation. The plant is not validated against vehicle data, the driver is modeled by a steering-angle source that does not react to the steering feel, and the current loop of the motor is not modeled. Section 2 presents the plant, Section 3 the problem, the reference and the assist limit, Section 4 the controllers, Section 5 the evaluation protocol, Section 6 the results, Section 7 the discussion and Section 8 the conclusion.

2. Plant model
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The plant consists of the steering column, the tires and the vehicle body, with the sensors and the motor around them. It is a nonlinear model with four states, built to reproduce one phenomenon: how the torque that the road returns to the steering wheel changes with the friction coefficient.
2.1. System and driver model
Figure 1 shows the C-EPS. The driver acts on the steering wheel, which is connected through an elastic torsion bar to the lower column. The torque sensor measures the twist of the bar. The assist motor acts on the lower column through a reduction gear, and a rack-and-pinion turns the rotation of the column into the motion of the tie rods and of the steered wheels. In the opposite direction, the lateral force of the front tires creates a torque about the kingpin axis, which returns to the column as the road reaction torque $T_r$.
[Figure 1: Mechanical structure of the column-type EPS | Figures/Figure_1_steering_column.png]
The model has three blocks (Figure 2): the steering column, the tires (an algebraic block without state of its own) and the vehicle body (single-track model with two degrees of freedom). Two feedback paths close it. The column angle $\theta_2$ sets the steered angle, hence the front lateral force $F_{yf}$ and the reaction torque $T_r$, which returns to the column. The lateral forces drive the sideslip angle $\beta$ and the yaw rate $\gamma$, which return to the tire block through the slip angles. Without both paths the model would lose the mechanism of over-assist.
[Figure 2: Block diagram of the plant: steering column, tires and vehicle body | Figures/Figure_2_plant.png]
The driver is modeled by the steering-wheel angle $\theta_1(t)$, a known input, as in [1] and [2]. If the driver torque $T_d$ were given instead, the steady-state torque balance would force $T_s = T_d$ whatever the assist, and the assist torque could not change the quantity to be controlled. The angle model corresponds to a driver who holds the angle regardless of the torque and does not react to the steering feel; it measures the cause of over-assist (the torque deviates from the desired value) but not the reaction of a human driver to it. The inputs of the model are the assist torque $T_a$ (control), the steering angle $\theta_1$ and the speed $v$ (exogenous, measured) and the friction coefficient $\mu$ (exogenous, not measured).
2.2. Steering column
The column is modeled as two inertias joined by a linear torsion bar [1]. With the steering angle $\theta_1$ given, the lower column obeys
  (1)  J_col*theta2_ddot = K*(theta1 - theta2) + T_a - T_r - C_col*theta2_dot - T_f*tanh(c*theta2_dot)
  [ J_{col}\ddot\theta_2=K(\theta_1-\theta_2)+T_a-T_r-C_{col}\dot\theta_2-T_f\tanh(c\,\dot\theta_2) ]
and the torque measured by the sensor is the torque transmitted by the bar:
  (2)  T_s = K*(theta1 - theta2)
  [ T_s=K(\theta_1-\theta_2) ]
Here $J_{col}$ is the inertia of the lower column, motor and rack reduced to the column, $C_{col}$ the viscous damping, $K$ the torsion stiffness and $T_f$ the amplitude of the Coulomb friction of the gears. The sign function of the Coulomb friction is smoothed by $\tanh(c\,\dot\theta_2)$ so that the equation is continuous at zero speed. In steady state, with $\dot\theta_2 = 0$, the torque balance reads
  (3)  T_s = T_r - T_a
  [ T_s=T_r-T_a ]
so the assist torque reduces the sensed torque directly, and a drop of $T_r$ at constant $T_a$ lowers $T_s$: over-assist. With the parameters of Table 1 the lower column alone has a natural frequency of 5.7 Hz and a damping ratio of 0.17. The road reaction acts as a spring in parallel with the torsion bar, with stiffness $k_r = e_{p0} C_{\alpha f}/n_{st}^2 \approx 30$ N.m/rad (about 21 % of $K$), so the coupled resonance is about 6 Hz; it limits the bandwidth of any torque feedback.
2.3. Tire and road reaction torque
The two wheels of an axle are lumped into one. With the small-angle assumption, the steered angle is $\delta_f = \theta_2/n_{st}$, where $n_{st}$ is the overall steering ratio, and the slip angles of the front and rear tires are
  (4)  alpha_f = theta2/n_st - beta - l_f*gamma/v,   alpha_r = -beta + l_r*gamma/v
  [ \alpha_f=\dfrac{\theta_2}{n_{st}}-\beta-\dfrac{l_f\gamma}{v},\qquad \alpha_r=-\beta+\dfrac{l_r\gamma}{v} ]
where $l_f$, $l_r$ are the distances from the center of gravity to the front and rear axle. The static axle loads are $F_{zf} = m g l_r/(l_f + l_r)$ and $F_{zr} = m g l_f/(l_f + l_r)$. The lateral force of each axle follows the Magic Formula of Pacejka [8]:
  (5)  F_y = D*sin(C*atan(B*alpha - E*(B*alpha - atan(B*alpha)))),   D = mu*F_z,   B = C_alpha/(C*D)
  [ F_y=D\sin\Big(C\arctan\big(B\alpha-E(B\alpha-\arctan(B\alpha))\big)\Big),\qquad D=\mu F_z,\quad B=\dfrac{C_\alpha}{C\,D} ]
The peak $D$ is proportional to the friction coefficient, and $B$ is chosen so that the initial slope $B C D$ equals the cornering stiffness $C_\alpha$, which does not depend on $\mu$. At small slip angles the force is therefore almost independent of $\mu$, and when $\mu$ drops the tire saturates earlier and lower. The geometry of the front wheel is shown in Figure 3. The kingpin axis meets the ground at the point $Q$, ahead of the center $C$ of the contact patch by the caster trail $e_c$, a fixed geometric length; the front lateral force acts at the point $P$, behind $C$, by the pneumatic trail $t$, which shrinks as the slip angle grows and vanishes when the patch slides completely [8]. The lever arm about the kingpin point is the sum $e_p = e_c + t$. For the pneumatic trail we use the linear law of [2]:
  (6)  e_p = e_c + max(0, t_0 - sgn(alpha_f)*t_0*C_alpha_f*tan(alpha_f)/(3*mu*F_zf)),   e_c = e_p0 - t_0
  [ e_p=e_c+\max\Big(0,\;t_0-\mathrm{sgn}(\alpha_f)\,t_0\dfrac{C_{\alpha f}\tan\alpha_f}{3\mu F_{zf}}\Big),\qquad e_c=e_{p0}-t_0 ]
where $t_0$ is the pneumatic trail at zero slip and $e_{p0}$ the total lever arm at zero slip. Conservation of power through the lossless steering linkage gives the reaction torque at the column,
  (7)  T_r = (e_p/n_st)*F_yf
  [ T_r=\dfrac{e_p}{n_{st}}F_{yf} ]
[Figure 3: Forces on the front wheel. (a) Top view: steered angle, velocity direction, slip angle, lateral force at P and its moment about the kingpin point Q. (b) Side view: contact patch, caster trail, pneumatic trail and total lever arm (not to scale) | Figures/Figure_3_tire_forces.png]
When $\mu$ drops, $T_r$ falls for two cumulative reasons: $F_{yf}$ saturates earlier and lower (Eq. (5)), and the pneumatic trail shrinks faster (Eq. (6)). This is the physical mechanism of over-assist in the model. The trail law (6) is a linear simplification of the brush model of the tire [8] (Figure 4). In the brush model the bristles of the contact patch deflect in proportion to the distance from the leading edge; they adhere until the lateral force per length reaches the friction limit, and slide behind that point. With a parabolic vertical load the sliding zone starts at the rear of the patch and grows with the slip, so the point of application of the force moves from one third of the half-length behind the center (the trail $t_0$) to the center when the whole patch slides, at $\tan\alpha_{sl}=3\mu F_{zf}/C_{\alpha f}$. Figure 4(d) compares the brush trail with the linear law: both start at $t_0$ and vanish at $\tan\alpha_{sl}$, but the brush trail is smaller in between (0.72 against 0.85 of $t_0$ at 15 % of $\tan\alpha_{sl}$, and 0.21 against 0.50 at half of it). The linear law therefore tends to underestimate how much the reaction torque falls when $\mu$ drops, so the over-assist of the model is, if anything, conservative.
[Figure 4: Brush model of the contact patch (travel to the right). (a)-(c) Lateral force per length, adhesion (blue) and sliding (red) zones, point of application of the lateral force Fy and pneumatic trail t for increasing slip; the dashed curve is the friction limit. (d) Normalized lateral force and pneumatic trail against slip, with the linear trail law of Eq. (6) and the Magic Formula of Eq. (5) with the same initial slope and peak | Figures/Figure_4_brush_model.png]
2.4. Vehicle body
With the speed $v$ treated as slowly varying, the single-track model gives
  (8)  a_y = (F_yf + F_yr)/m,   beta_dot = a_y/v - gamma,   gamma_dot = (l_f*F_yf - l_r*F_yr)/I_z
  [ a_y=\dfrac{F_{yf}+F_{yr}}{m},\qquad \dot\beta=\dfrac{a_y}{v}-\gamma,\qquad \dot\gamma=\dfrac{l_f\,F_{yf}-l_r\,F_{yr}}{I_z} ]
Since each lateral force is bounded by its peak $D$, the lateral acceleration satisfies $a_y \le \mu g$ in every state.
2.5. State-space form and parameters
The model has four states, $x = [\theta_2,\ \dot\theta_2,\ \beta,\ \gamma]^T$, and is affine in the control:
  (9)  x_dot = f(x, theta_1, v, mu) + g*T_a,   g = [0, 1/J_col, 0, 0]^T
  [ \dot x=f(x,\theta_1,v,\mu)+g\,T_a,\qquad g=\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1/J_{col} & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}^T ]
where $f$ collects Eqs. (1)-(8). The outputs are the sensed torque $T_s$, the lateral acceleration $a_y$, the column speed $\dot\theta_2$ and the yaw rate $\gamma$; all are measured on a car except $\beta$. The parameters are in Table 1. The column parameters were identified on a passenger car in [1]; the vehicle and the tire (mass, inertia, cornering stiffness) come from the same sedan data set of [9], and $C$, $E$ from the illustrative values of [8]. Three values are our choice: $n_{st} = 16$ (the ratios measured on three cars in [7] are 16.5, 19 and 24.7), $e_{p0} = 0.048$ m, and the split $t_0 = 0.025$ m, $e_c = 0.023$ m, taken as the middle of the 17-35 mm range that the brush model of [8] gives for the pneumatic trail before any simulation was run.

2.6. Sensors and motor
The controller sees measured signals only. Each measurement is the true value plus white noise, quantized at the resolution of the sensor and held at its update period (Table 2). The standard deviation of the noise is one resolution step, except for the speed (0.38 km/h, from [10]). The torque, steering-angle and column-speed signals are read directly by the EPS controller every 1 ms; the speed, yaw rate and lateral acceleration come from the stability controller over the CAN bus, with an assumed update period of 10 ms [to be sourced]. The controller runs at 1 ms. The motor is a first-order lag $G_m(s)=\omega_m/(s+\omega_m)$ with a bandwidth of 100 Hz [1]; its current loop and its power limit are not modeled, and the assist torque is bounded only by $T_{a,max}(v)$ of Section 3.

2.7. Validity and limitations
The model is used for speeds of 20-100 km/h and friction coefficients of 0.2-0.8. Below 20 km/h the terms divided by $v$ and the static-tire assumption break down. The small-angle assumption limits the lateral acceleration to the smaller of $\mu g$ and the value at which the steered angle or the sideslip reaches 10 degrees; at 20 km/h this is about 0.2 g for every friction coefficient, at 25 km/h 0.31 g on dry road, and from 45 km/h upward $\mu g$. Open-loop simulations with $T_a = 0$ show that load transfer changes every indicator by less than 1 % and that tire lag mainly slows the body response at low speed, while the torque at the wheel is almost unaffected. The size of over-assist depends on the unmeasured split of the lever arm: for a friction drop from 0.8 to 0.3 with the wheel held at 0.2 g and no assist, the sensed torque falls by about 9-10 % with the chosen $t_0$, between 6 % and 13 % over the whole 17-35 mm range, and between 1-3 % and 18 % at the two physical bounds $t_0 = 0$ and $t_0 = e_{p0}$. The size of over-assist in this paper is therefore reliable only as an order of magnitude; its direction (a lighter wheel when $\mu$ drops) does not depend on $t_0$. Further limitations: the linear trail law (6) is not the brush trail (Figure 4(d)), the speed is constant in the body equations, both sides of the car have the same friction, there is no roll, the motor has no dynamics beyond the lag, and the driver does not react to the torque. The model has not been compared with measurements of a vehicle.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. Problem formulation
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1. Control objective and information sets
The controlled variable is the sensed torque $T_s$, the torque that the driver feels, and the control is the assist torque $T_a$. By Eq. (3), $T_a$ lowers $T_s$ in steady state; the column angle shifts and changes $T_r$ a little, so the drop of $T_s$ is not exactly the rise of $T_a$, but the sign of the action is fixed. Because $\mu$ is not measured, any change of $T_r$ caused by the road shows up only in $T_s$, and a controller that must remove it needs feedback of $T_s$. Two classes of controllers are considered, and they differ only in the signals they read (Figure 5). The assist map, with its compensator, reads $T_s$ and $v$. The torque-feedback controllers (PI, PID) read $T_s$, $v$ and $a_y$ and compute the reference of Section 3.2 internally. Neither class reads $\mu$.
[Figure 5: Closed loop shared by all controllers: scenario, plant, sensors, controller, assist limit and motor; true and measured signals | Figures/Figure_5_architecture.png]
3.2. Reference torque
The reference $T_{d,ref}(v, a_y)$ is the torque that an average driver prefers at the speed $v$ and the lateral acceleration $a_y$. We use the ideal steering-wheel torque of [3] (Table 4 there), given at five speeds from 20 to 100 km/h and four levels of lateral acceleration from 0.1 g to 0.4 g (Table 3). The torque grows with the lateral acceleration, because a sharper turn should feel heavier, and with the speed, because the wheel should be heavier at high speed. [7] measured the ideal torque on three cars of 1.5, 1.7 and 1.9 t and found it almost independent of the vehicle for similar size and mass; the car of [3] (about 1.53 t) is within 3 % of the mass of the model car, so the table is used without correction.
Table 3: Ideal steering-wheel torque $T_{d,ref}$ [N.m] from [3]
$a_y$ [g] | 20 km/h | 40 km/h | 60 km/h | 80 km/h | 100 km/h
0.1 | 1.0 | 1.7 | 2.1 | 2.3 | 2.6
0.2 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 2.4 | 2.8 | 3.0
0.3 | 1.6 | 2.3 | 2.8 | 3.1 | 3.3
0.4 | 1.8 | 2.4 | 2.9 | 3.3 | 3.4
Three completions make the table a usable reference. The point $a_y = 0$ with zero torque is added, because a car driving straight returns no road torque. The table is interpolated by monotone piecewise cubic Hermite polynomials in $a_y$ and then in $v$, so the reference is smooth and passes through every value of the table. The table is extended to negative $a_y$ by symmetry, and outside the table the boundary value is held (speed outside 20-100 km/h, $|a_y|$ above 0.4 g):
  (10)  T_d,ref(v, a_y) = sgn(a_y)*T_hat(v, |a_y|),   T_hat(v, 0) = 0
  [ T_{d,ref}(v,a_y)=\mathrm{sgn}(a_y)\,\hat T(v,|a_y|),\qquad \hat T(v,0)=0 ]
where $\hat T$ is the interpolant of Table 3. The reference depends only on measured signals and is continuous when the direction of the turn changes. The essential choice is that it depends on $a_y$ and not on $\mu$: on a slippery road the car reaches a lower lateral acceleration, and a lighter wheel at that acceleration is information for the driver, not an error.
3.3. Tracking error and over-assist
The tracking error is the difference between the sensed torque and the reference,
  (11)  e_T = T_s - T_d,ref(v, a_y)
  [ e_T=T_s-T_{d,ref}(v,a_y) ]
and the control goal is $e_T = 0$ at every speed and lateral acceleration. Because the reference varies with $v$ and $a_y$, this is a tracking problem and not the regulation of a constant. Over-assist occurs when $\mathrm{sgn}(a_y)\,e_T < 0$, that is, $|T_s| < |T_{d,ref}|$: the wheel is lighter than desired at the same speed and lateral acceleration. The opposite sign is a lack of assist. The relative form of the error used for scoring is defined with the controllers in Section 4.
3.4. Why an assist map cannot remove the friction-induced error
Let the map be $T_a = M(v, T_s)$ and consider a steady state. By Eq. (3), $T_s$ solves
  (12)  T_s + M(v, T_s) = T_r(v, a_y, mu)
  [ T_s+M(v,T_s)=T_r(v,a_y,\mu) ]
The left side does not contain $\mu$. The calibration on dry road makes $T_s$ equal to $T_{d,ref}(v, a_y)$ at $\mu = 0.8$. For the same $v$ and $a_y$, a lower friction coefficient reduces the right side by $\Delta T_r < 0$ (Section 2.3), and to first order the sensed torque changes by
  (13)  Delta T_s = Delta T_r/(1 + s),   s = dM/dT_s
  [ \Delta T_s=\dfrac{\Delta T_r}{1+s},\qquad s=\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial T_s} ]
Because $T_{d,ref}(v, a_y)$ does not change at the same $a_y$, $\Delta T_s$ is the steady over-assist. A map therefore attenuates the error by the factor $1/(1+s)$ and can do so only by raising its slope $s$. As an example, at 100 km/h and $a_y = 0.2$ g the model gives $T_r = 5.22$ N.m on dry road and 4.75 N.m at $\mu = 0.3$, so $\Delta T_r = -0.47$ N.m ($-9$ %), which is also the drop of $T_s$ without assist. The calibrated map has the slope limited to $s = K_{max} = 6$ at that point (the natural slope is 6.48), so Eq. (13) predicts $\Delta T_s = -0.067$ N.m, $-2.2$ % of $T_{d,ref} = 3.0$ N.m. A map with a high slope therefore already makes the steady over-assist small at moderate lateral acceleration. The slope cannot be raised at will: it raises the loop gain at the column resonance of about 6 Hz and the gain from sensor noise to the assist torque, so stability and noise limit it (Section 6). In contrast, a controller with integral action on $e_T$ can be at rest only when $e_T = 0$, provided $T_a$ is inside its limit. Because the assist needed, $T_{a,req} = T_r - T_{d,ref}$, falls when $T_r$ falls, the limit of Section 3.5 is not exceeded. Its steady over-assist is therefore zero by construction, whatever $\mu$ is; the price is paid in the dynamic behavior and in the noise gain of the added terms. Eq. (13) is derived in the smoothed-friction model, where the dry friction acts only while the column moves.
3.5. Assist limit
The assist torque is bounded for every controller by $T_{a,max}(v)$. In steady state, Eq. (3) gives the assist needed to reach the reference, $T_{a,req} = T_r(v, a_y, \mu) - T_{d,ref}(v, a_y)$. $T_r$ is largest on dry road, so the largest need is evaluated at $\mu = 0.8$ over the lateral accelerations that both the model and the reference allow, $a_{y,lim}(v) = \min(a_{y,max}(v, 0.8),\ 0.4\,g)$. This follows the way [2] and [5] size the assist from the difference between the largest resisting torque and the desired torque. We add a margin $F$ for the dry friction of the column, an uncertainty with a known bound: following Slotine and Li [11] (Sec. 7.1.3), the magnitude of the control must exceed the nominal need by the bound of the model error. The margin of the control law itself is not added, because it belongs to the controller and not to the motor.
  (14)  T_a,max(v) = max over a_y of [T_r(v, a_y, 0.8) - T_d,ref(v, a_y)] + F,   F = T_f
  [ T_{a,max}(v)=\max_{a_y}\big[T_r(v,a_y,0.8)-T_{d,ref}(v,a_y)\big]+F,\qquad F=T_f ]
Here the maximum is taken over $0 \le a_y \le a_{y,lim}(v)$. Table 4 gives sample values; the limit is interpolated linearly in $v$ and applied in the motor block, not in the controllers. The motor capacity is not modeled (no data), so the limit is a model-based estimate and its size is reliable only as an order of magnitude; what matters for the comparison is that all controllers share the same limit.

3.6. Problem statement
Given the plant of Eq. (9) with the sensors of Table 2 and the motor of Section 2.6, find a controller $T_a = u(\text{measured signals})$ with $|T_a| \le T_{a,max}(v)$ such that $e_T$ of Eq. (11) is small for every friction coefficient in 0.2-0.8 and every speed in 20-100 km/h, while the loop satisfies two requirements, made precise as gates in Section 5: (i) the closed loop is stable with a margin, and (ii) the steady error on a dry road is small. The fraction of time in which the assist pushes against the hand of the driver (reverse assist) and the switching of the command between its two limits because of sensor noise (bang-bang) are reported but not gated. The tracking error and the noise of the command pull in opposite directions, so the result of a design is a point in the plane (tracking error, command noise), and controllers are compared by their whole front and not by one tuned point.

4. Controller design
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1. Common structure and design flow
Three controllers are designed: the assist map with a lead-lag compensator (Map), a PI controller and a PID controller on the torque error. All read their inputs from the measured bus of Figure 5 and return the command $T_{a,cmd}$ at a sample time of 1 ms; none clamps its output, since the limit $T_{a,max}(v)$ is applied by the motor block (Section 3.5). Only the structure of each controller is part of the comparison: no controller filters the measured torque, uses a notch filter or a feedforward from the steering angle, so that what one controller may do the others may also do.
Every controller is built in the same three stages. (i) The structure is fixed and the parts that need no tuning are computed from the plant: the calibration table of the map, the anti-windup gain of the PI and PID. (ii) A linear analysis of the loop fixes what can be fixed without simulation and bounds the search: the compensator of the map, the stability region and the integral-time limits of the PI and PID. (iii) The free parameters that remain are swept on one calibration case with sensor noise; every point is simulated, the gates of Section 5 are applied and one point is selected by the rule of Section 5. A grid is refined around the best point, and widened when the best point lies on its edge, so that the selected point is interior to the region searched.
The steady accuracy of a controller in a window where the speed and the reference are constant is measured by the relative error
  (15)  e_rel = 100*sgn(T_d,ref)*mean(e_T)/mean(|T_d,ref|)
  [ e_{rel}=100\,\mathrm{sgn}(T_{d,ref})\,\dfrac{\mathrm{mean}(e_T)}{\mathrm{mean}(|T_{d,ref}|)} ]
where the means are taken over the window. By the definition of Section 3.3, $e_{rel} < 0$ is over-assist and $e_{rel} > 0$ a wheel heavier than desired. The measure is not used when the mean reference in the window is close to zero (windows with a steering reversal), where the denominator makes it meaningless; the root mean square of $e_T$ is used there. The accuracy gate of Section 5 limits the largest $|e_{rel}|$ over the steady windows of the calibration case.
4.2. Assist map with compensator
The map follows the structure of [1]: a static table of the assist torque in the measured torque and the speed, followed by a lead-lag compensator that stabilizes the loop. It reads only $T_s$ and $v$. It is built in seven steps.
Step 1, calibration points. The table is calibrated on the dry road. At rest ($\dot\theta_2 = 0$) Eq. (3) gives $T_s = T_r - T_a$, so to make the sensed torque equal to the reference the map must deliver $T_r - T_{d,ref}$. For every speed from 20 to 100 km/h in steps of 5 km/h (17 speeds) and every lateral acceleration from 0.1 g, the lowest row of Table 3, to $\min(a_{y,max}(v, 0.8),\ 0.4\,g)$ in steps of 0.005 g, the steady cornering state of the plant is solved and gives the road torque $T_r(v, a_y, 0.8)$; the reference is interpolated as in the reference block. Each point of the calibration is the pair
  (16)  T_s,cal = T_d,ref(v, a_y),   T_a,cal = T_r(v, a_y, 0.8) - T_d,ref(v, a_y)
  [ T_{s,cal}=T_{d,ref}(v,a_y),\qquad T_{a,cal}=T_r(v,a_y,0.8)-T_{d,ref}(v,a_y) ]
and the curve through these points at one speed, the natural curve, is the map that makes the dry-road steady error zero. The points do not depend on the controller parameters and are computed once.
Step 2, slope limit and dead zone. The slope of the natural curve exceeds 8 over part of the curve at every speed above 20 km/h, and the slope of the map is the gain of the loop (Section 3.4), so the curve is built with a slope limit $K_{max}$, the same at every speed. Below the dead zone $|T_s| \le T_{s0} = 0.3$ N.m the map gives no assist, so that the wheel is not assisted around the center. With the breakpoints $T_{s,0} = T_{s0}$ and $T_{s,k}$, $k \ge 1$, the values of Eq. (16) in increasing order, the curve is
  (17)  M_0 = 0,   M_k = max(0, min(T_a,cal,k, M_(k-1) + K_max*(T_s,k - T_s,k-1))),   k >= 1
  [ M_0=0,\qquad M_k=\max\big(0,\ \min\big(T_{a,cal,k},\ M_{k-1}+K_{max}\,(T_{s,k}-T_{s,k-1})\big)\big),\quad k\ge 1 ]
so it follows the natural curve where the slope of that curve is below $K_{max}$ and a line of slope $K_{max}$ elsewhere; where the slope is limited the curve lies below the calibration points and leaves a steady error on the dry road, which the accuracy gate bounds.
Step 3, lookup table. The curve of each speed is sampled at $|T_s|$ from 0 to 5 N.m in steps of 0.005 N.m, with $M = 0$ in the dead zone and the last value held beyond the last breakpoint. The result is a table in $(v, |T_s|)$, read with linear interpolation in both inputs, and the map output is
  (18)  T_a,map = sgn(T_s)*M(v, |T_s|)
  [ T_{a,map}=\mathrm{sgn}(T_s)\,M(v,|T_s|) ]
Step 4, linearized plants. The map in series with the plant has a high gain, so the loop would vibrate or diverge [1]; the compensator is designed on linear models. The plant of Section 2 is linearized from $T_a$ to $T_s$ around steady cornering, with the steering angle held by the driver, at four operating points per speed: nearly straight ($a_y \approx 0$) and cornering at 0.3 g on the dry road ($\mu = 0.8$), and nearly straight and cornering at 0.15 g on a slippery road ($\mu = 0.2$). With the 17 speeds this gives 68 linear plants $G(s)$. The Coulomb friction is not linearized: its slope at rest is very large and it only adds damping once the column moves, so the linear model keeps the viscous damping alone, which is the less damped and therefore the conservative case.
Step 5, compensator. The map output passes through a lead-lag compensator
  (19)  T_a,cmd = C(s)*T_a,map,   C(s) = (s/z + 1)/(s/p + 1) * (s/z_1 + 1)/(s/p_1 + 1)
  [ T_{a,cmd}=C(s)\,T_{a,map},\qquad C(s)=\dfrac{s/z+1}{s/p+1}\cdot\dfrac{s/z_1+1}{s/p_1+1} ]
with $p > z$ (lead) and $p_1 < z_1$ (lag); both stages have unit gain at zero frequency, so the steady accuracy of the map is unchanged. The lead compensates the phase lag of the motor, of the delay and of the column resonance; the lag lowers the gain above its pole, which cuts the amplification of sensor noise. The local slope of the map lies anywhere between zero and $K_{max}$, so the loop is checked at the slopes $k = K_{max}\{0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1\}$:
  (20)  L(jw) = -k*C(jw)*G_m(jw)*exp(-jw*T)*G(jw)
  [ L(j\omega)=-k\,C(j\omega)\,G_m(j\omega)\,e^{-j\omega T}\,G(j\omega) ]
where $G_m$ is the motor lag of Section 2.6 and $T = 1$ ms the computation delay. A candidate $(z, p, z_1, p_1)$ is admissible when, for all 68 plants and all four slopes, the closed loop is stable by the Nyquist criterion, the phase margin is at least 45 degrees at every gain crossover and the gain margin is at least 2. The lag must also leave the band of the driver, 0.2-2 Hz [4], nearly untouched: its gain at 2 Hz is held above a level $g_{min}$,
  (21)  |(j*w_2/z_1 + 1)/(j*w_2/p_1 + 1)| >= g_min,   w_2 = 2*pi*2 rad/s
  [ \left|\dfrac{j\omega_2/z_1+1}{j\omega_2/p_1+1}\right|\ \ge\ g_{min},\qquad \omega_2=2\pi\cdot 2\ \mathrm{rad/s} ]
and $g_{min} = 1$ removes the lag (lead only). Among the admissible candidates we take the one with the smallest high-frequency gain
  (22)  K_HF = K_max*(p/z)*(p_1/z_1)
  [ K_{HF}=K_{max}\,\dfrac{p}{z}\,\dfrac{p_1}{z_1} ]
because this gain sets how much sensor noise on $T_s$ reaches the assist torque; ties go to the larger phase margin. The search runs on a coarse grid over $z$ = 5-500 rad/s, $p/z$ = 1.2-300 with $p \le \pi/T$, $z_1$ = 8-120 rad/s, $p_1/z_1$ = 0.1-0.9 with $p_1 \ge 3$ rad/s, then on a grid four times finer around the best coarse candidate; a best candidate on a bound of the grid would require the bound to be widened. The margins of the chosen compensator are checked again on a finer frequency grid.
Step 6, design table. Step 5 gives one compensator for each pair $(K_{max}, g_{min})$. It is run for eleven levels $g_{min}$ = 0.25-1.0 and slopes $K_{max}$ from 5 upward, 103 pairs in all, of which 98 have an admissible compensator. Each compensator is discretized by the Tustin rule at 1 ms.
Step 7, selection. Two numbers remain free, $K_{max}$ and $g_{min}$; the compensator follows from them. 61 pairs from the design table are simulated on the calibration case, each with its own compensator. Because the linear limit of Step 5 might be conservative, twelve further runs raise the slope of the table above the $K_{max}$ for which the compensator was designed. The gates and the selection rule of Section 5 are applied to the measured pairs, with the band constraint of Section 5 on the actual lag gain at 2 Hz; the lead-only variant ($g_{min} = 1$) is selected by the same rule as a control. Table 5 gives the selected map.

4.3. PI and PID torque feedback
The torque-feedback controllers read $T_s$, $v$ and $a_y$ and compute the reference of Eq. (10) from the measured $v$ and $a_y$, so they act on the error $e_T$ of Eq. (11). A positive $e_T$ (the wheel is heavier than desired) calls for more assist, so the gains are positive. They are built in six steps.
Step 1, control law. The PID law is
  (23)  T_a,cmd = K_p*e_T + K_i*int(e_T) dt + K_d*s/(T_f*s + 1)*T_s
  [ T_{a,cmd}=K_p\,e_T+K_i\!\int e_T\,dt+K_d\,\dfrac{s}{T_f\,s+1}\,T_s ]
The derivative acts on the measured torque, not on $e_T$, so that a change of the reference does not produce a kick, and it is filtered by a first-order lag of time constant $T_f$ to limit the amplification of sensor noise [12]. The PI controller is the same law with $K_d = 0$.
Step 2, discrete form and anti-windup. The law is computed at $T = 1$ ms. With $u_I$ the integral term and $d$ the derivative term,
  (24)  d[k] = a*d[k-1] + b*(T_s[k] - T_s[k-1]),   u_I[k+1] = u_I[k] + T*(K_i*e_T[k] + K_aw*(T_a,lim[k] - T_a,cmd[k]))
  [ d[k]=a\,d[k-1]+b\,\big(T_s[k]-T_s[k-1]\big),\qquad u_I[k+1]=u_I[k]+T\big(K_i\,e_T[k]+K_{aw}\,(T_{a,lim}[k]-T_{a,cmd}[k])\big) ]
with $a = T_f/(T_f + T)$, $b = K_d/(T_f + T)$ and $T_{a,cmd}[k] = K_p e_T[k] + u_I[k] + d[k]$. The integrator uses the forward Euler rule. The term in $K_{aw}$ is back-calculation anti-windup: $T_{a,lim}$ is the command after the limit of the motor block, so the term is zero while the limit is inactive and pulls the integrator back when it acts. The gain is fixed at $K_{aw} = K_i/K_p$, a tracking time equal to the integral time $T_i = K_p/K_i$, and is not tuned.
Step 3, linear design model. For the analysis the steering angle is held and the vehicle body is replaced by its effect on the column, the road stiffness $k_r$ of Section 2.2, which gives
  (25)  G(s) = K/(J_col*s^2 + C_col*s + K + k_r) * G_m(s),   k_r = e_p0*C_alpha_f/n_st^2 (dry) or k_r = 0 (saturated tire)
  [ G(s)=\dfrac{K}{J_{col}s^2+C_{col}s+K+k_r}\,G_m(s),\qquad k_r=\dfrac{e_{p0}C_{\alpha f}}{n_{st}^2}\ \text{(dry)}\ \text{or}\ k_r=0\ \text{(saturated tire)} ]
The two cases bound the road stiffness: small slip on a dry road, and a tire at its friction limit, which returns no extra torque for extra steering. The resonance is at 39.7 rad/s (6.3 Hz) with a damping ratio of 0.15 on the dry road and at 36.1 rad/s with 0.17 for the saturated tire. The model was checked against step responses of the assist torque on the nonlinear plant at 30, 60 and 100 km/h: the ringing frequency agrees (6.1-6.2 Hz against 6.2 Hz) and the root-mean-square difference of the responses is 2-11 %, largest at 30 km/h, where the model underestimates the static gain (0.89 against 1.03).
Step 4, stability region. The discrete loop of the model, with a zero-order hold at 1 ms, one sample of computation delay and both road cases, is tested for closed-loop stability under the same stress as the margin gate of Section 5: loop gain doubled and one extra sample of delay. This gives the largest $K_i$ for every $K_p$, for the PI and, for the PID, for every $(K_d, T_f)$. Points outside this region would fail the margin gate, so the simulated grids are restricted to it.
Step 5, integral-time and filter limits. The ultimate gain and period of the loop under proportional control, found on the same discrete model as the smallest ultimate gain and largest period of the two road cases, are $K_{cu} = 3.10$ and $P_u = 87$ ms. The integral time is not allowed below the Ziegler-Nichols setting [12] (Table 12.7), which bounds the integral action from above:
  (26)  T_i = K_p/K_i >= P_u/1.2 (PI),   T_i >= P_u/2 (PID),   T_f >= 0.05*T_d with T_d = K_d/K_p (PID)
  [ T_i=\dfrac{K_p}{K_i}\ \ge\ \dfrac{P_u}{1.2}\ \text{(PI)},\qquad T_i\ \ge\ \dfrac{P_u}{2}\ \text{(PID)},\qquad T_f\ \ge\ 0.05\,T_d,\quad T_d=\dfrac{K_d}{K_p}\ \text{(PID)} ]
that is, $T_i \ge 72.5$ ms for the PI and $T_i \ge 43.5$ ms for the PID. The last condition keeps the derivative filter within the range $T_f = (0.05\text{-}0.2)\,T_d$ given in [12] (Eq. 8-12) at its lower end. The band constraint of Section 5 asks the derivative filter to pass the band of the driver, a cutoff $1/(2\pi T_f) \ge 2$ Hz, that is $T_f \le 79.6$ ms; the PI has no filter.
Step 6, selection. The free parameters are $(K_p, K_i)$ for the PI and $(K_p, K_i, K_d, T_f)$ for the PID. Each grid starts coarse inside the region of Step 4, is widened where the best point lies on its edge, and is refined around the best point with relative steps. 453 points of the PI and 824 points of the PID were simulated on the calibration case. The gates and the selection rule of Section 5 are applied with the limits of Eq. (26). Table 6 gives the selected values. The map has two tuned parameters ($K_{max}$, $g_{min}$), the PI two and the PID four.

5. Calibration and evaluation protocol
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The protocol was fixed before the final runs and is the same for every controller: one calibration case on which the free parameters are chosen, two measures, two gates, one selection rule, and a set of scoring cases that are not used for tuning. The steering angle of the driver is a prescribed signal computed from the target speed and lateral acceleration (Section 2.1); it does not depend on the controller, so all controllers receive the same input, and every difference in the results comes from the controller. All runs use the noisy sensors of Table 2. The first 2 s of every case, the start from rest, are not scored.
5.1. Calibration case
The calibration case CALIB (117 s) is built from the working range of the vehicle and not from the ability of any controller. It consists of three speed blocks, 20 km/h (0-33 s), 60 km/h (33-80 s) and 100 km/h (80-117 s), joined by short straight runs that carry the speed change. The road is dry ($\mu = 0.8$) except for one friction step. Each block contains, in this order: a staircase of steady corners, each level reached in 1 s and held for 3 s, with the last second of the hold as the evaluation window, so that a slow integrator is not mistaken for a steady error; a sine steering around a base level at 0.5 Hz for four cycles; and small corrections of $\pm 0.03$ g around the same base, as in lane keeping. The 60 km/h block ends with a hold at 0.3 g during which $\mu$ falls to 0.6 at 71 s and returns to 0.8 at 75 s; the windows are the 2 s after each step and measure the response, not a steady state. At 20 km/h the staircase stops at 0.18 g because the plant reaches only about 0.2 g there. The windows at 0.4 g lie in the extreme region where the reference table is flat and the assist limit is tight; they are reported but enter neither the tracking measure nor the accuracy gate, so that the tuning is driven by the normal range of driving. 5.2. Measures
The tracking measure $R$ is the root mean square of the tracking error in each group of windows of Table 7, relative to the mean reference of the group, averaged over the four groups so that each kind of driving counts equally:
  (27)  R = (1/4) * sum over g in {static, sine, small, mu} of 100*RMS_g(e_T)/mean_g(|T_d,ref|)
  [ R=\dfrac{1}{4}\sum_{g\in\{\mathrm{static},\,\mathrm{sine},\,\mathrm{small},\,\mathrm{mu}\}}100\,\dfrac{\mathrm{RMS}_g(e_T)}{\mathrm{mean}_g(|T_{d,ref}|)} ]
The noise measure $S$ is the root mean square of the assist torque after a first-order high-pass filter with a cutoff of 5 Hz, over the whole case:
  (28)  S = RMS( T_a - F_5(T_a) ),   F_5(s) = 1/(s/(2*pi*5) + 1)
  [ S=\mathrm{RMS}\big(T_a-F_5(T_a)\big),\qquad F_5(s)=\dfrac{1}{s/(2\pi\cdot 5)+1} ]
The driver steers below 5 Hz [4], so the content of the assist torque above this frequency carries no steering command; it is felt as vibration and comes mainly from sensor noise amplified by the controller. $R$ and $S$ pull in opposite directions. Three further quantities are reported but not gated: the total variation of the assist torque per second, the fraction of time with reverse assist, that is, $|T_s| \ge 0.5$ N.m with the assist torque opposite in sign to $T_s$ (0.5 N.m lies in the range 0.44-0.58 N.m of the tactile threshold of the steering torque [14], citing [15]), and the fraction of time in which the command switches between the two limits because of noise (bang-bang).
5.3. Gates
A point of a parameter grid is admissible only when it passes two gates, both fixed before the runs.
Stability margin. The calibration case is run twice more, once with the gain of the motor doubled and once with one extra computation delay of 1 ms. The closed loop must stay stable in both runs and $R$ must not grow to more than twice its nominal value. The factor 2 on the gain lies in the range 1.7-4.0 recommended for the gain margin in [12] (Sec. 14.7), and the extra delay is one more sample of the 1 ms controller. The factor 2 on $R$ is our choice; with factors 1.5 and 3 the selected points do not change.
Steady accuracy. The largest $|e_{rel}|$ of Eq. (15) over the nine static windows must not exceed 5 %. The just noticeable difference of the steering-wheel torque measured on 18 drivers is 10.2-18 % at 1-5 N.m [13], and the reference of Table 3 lies between 1.0 and 3.4 N.m, so 5 % is below half the smallest difference a driver can perceive.
5.4. Selection rule
Among the admissible points we select the one that minimizes the balance cost
  (29)  J = R * S
  [ J=R\,S ]
subject to a band constraint: any lag or filter inside the controller keeps a gain of at least $1/\sqrt{2}$ (-3 dB) at 2 Hz, the upper edge of the band of the driver, 0.2-2 Hz [4]. For the map this is the lag stage of Eq. (21), for the PID the derivative filter ($T_f \le 79.6$ ms); the PI has no filter. Without the constraint the cost $J$ of the map has no interior minimum: a deeper lag lowers $S$ faster than $R$ grows, and the map would be slowed down into the band of the driver. The same calibration case, gates, selection rule and grid refinement are applied to every controller (Section 4.1); the map has two tuned parameters, the PI two and the PID four.
5.5. Comparison
The main comparison is between the whole Pareto fronts of the admissible points in the plane $(S, R)$ and not between two tuned points. If the front of one controller lies below that of another over the common range of $S$, it is better under every selection rule; if the fronts cross, the crossing is reported; if the ranges of $S$ do not overlap, the controllers are not directly comparable. Differences in a scoring measure smaller than 5 % are treated as ties.
5.6. Scoring cases
Five scoring cases, none of them used for tuning, are run with the same noise sequence for every controller. Each case asks one question and has one main measure (Table 8). Scenes within a case are joined by straight runs in which the speed changes with a longitudinal acceleration of at most 2.2 m/s², so the assumption of slowly varying speed of the vehicle model holds. RAMP and EDGE are reported but are not part of the score.

7. Discussion and limitations
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7.1. Why the map falls short
The results confirm the mechanism of Section 3.4 and add a second one. First, the slope limit: the map attenuates a change of the road torque only by $1/(1+s)$ with $s \le K_{max} = 6$, so a friction drop leaves a steady over-assist ($-4.1$ % in SLIP), and where the natural slope of the calibration curve exceeds $K_{max}$ (low speed and high lateral acceleration, the extreme region) the map cannot reach its own calibration points (DRY at 40 km/h, EDGE). Raising $K_{max}$ is limited by the compensator: the front of Figure 6 flattens near $R = 25$ %. Second, the dry friction of the column. In RAMP the steering angle changes slowly and continuously, so the Coulomb friction of Eq. (1) is saturated at $\pm T_f = \pm 2$ N.m with a sign set by the direction of motion; a change of 2 N.m in the torque balance passes to $T_s$ through the same factor $1/(1+s)$ and, where the operating point leaves the sloped part of the table, almost undivided. The sign flips with the direction of the speed change, which is why the error is positive on the falling part and negative on the rising part, and why averaging the two does not cancel it for a nonlinear map. In a preliminary run at a mean deceleration of 0.15 m/s² instead of 0.5 m/s² the deviation was the same, so the effect is the friction and not a lag. An integral action on $e_T$ removes any constant torque in the balance, friction included, which is why the PI and the PID stay within 0.5 %.
7.2. Limits of the calibration case
The calibration case has steady windows at 20, 60 and 100 km/h only. The map passes the accuracy gate there (3.2 %) but leaves 13.0 % in DRY at 40 km/h and 0.3 g. A steady-state analysis of the map over the whole speed range locates the weak region at 25-45 km/h and 0.25-0.3 g, where the reference is nearly flat in $v$ while the road torque still grows, so the natural slope of the calibration curve (14-16) exceeds $K_{max}$. A calibration case with steady windows every 10 km/h would close this gap but would push the map to a larger $K_{max}$, hence a larger $S$; we kept the case fixed, as the protocol requires, and report the gap. The controllers with integral action are not affected, because they do not rely on a table calibrated at a few speeds.
7.3. Cost of feedback
Torque feedback does not come for free. At the selected points the PID has a larger $S$ than the map, and the PI and the PID push against the driver for a few percent of the time, about a quarter of the time in continuous steering. A plausible cause, not yet checked window by window, is that when the steering reverses $T_s$ crosses zero earlier than the integral term changes sign, so for a short time the assist opposes the sensed torque; whether this is felt by a driver needs a study with drivers in the loop. The Pareto fronts show that the higher noise of the selected PID is a choice of the selection rule and not a property of the structure: at equal $S$ the PID tracks better than the map.
7.4. Limitations
The study is limited to simulation. The plant is not validated against vehicle data; the driver is a steering-angle source that does not react to the steering feel; the current loop of the motor and the capacity of the motor are not modeled, so the assist limit is a model-based estimate. The reference is taken from one source [3]. Each controller was tuned with one noise sequence and all were scored with one common sequence; the spread over several sequences is not reported. The grids of the three controllers differ in size (61, 453 and 824 points), although each was refined until the selected point was interior. The map has a dead zone and the PI and PID do not, which may explain part of the difference in reverse assist.
