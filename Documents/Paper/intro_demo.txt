1. Introduction

Electric power steering (EPS) has largely replaced hydraulic steering in passenger cars because of its efficiency and the ease of tuning the steering feel [1]. In column-type EPS (C-EPS), the core of the controller is a nonlinear assist map that converts the measured driver torque and the vehicle speed into motor torque, complemented by a compensator that stabilizes the loop [1]. The map is normally tuned on a high-friction road. When the tire–road friction drops, the lateral tire force and the pneumatic trail decrease and the steering reaction torque falls with them; if the map is unchanged, the assistance becomes excessive and the steering wheel feels lighter than intended, which reduces the road feedback available to the driver [2,3]. Because drivers can notice torque changes of roughly 10–18% at the levels of 1–5 N·m typical of EPS-assisted cars [4], a deviation matters in practice only when it exceeds this order of magnitude.

What the steering-wheel torque should be is itself well studied. Steering-feel research relates the steering-wheel torque to the vehicle speed and to the lateral acceleration, and drivers prefer an effort that changes with speed [5]; tables of ideal torque indexed by speed and lateral acceleration are used to design assist characteristics [3]. Since lateral acceleration is already measured on the vehicle, a torque reference built from speed and lateral acceleration does not need an explicit friction value, and it gives a quantity that a controller can track directly.

Existing work follows four directions. First, the assist characteristic is designed from the steady-state inverse of the vehicle and steering dynamics [6]; such a design is calibrated for one nominal condition and does not adapt when friction changes. Second, friction-aware maps are selected or corrected from friction information: a neural network identifies the adhesion coefficient and selects between an urban and a rural map [3]; the adhesion coefficient and the front-axle load are given as inputs to a network that sets the map [2]; and the assist coefficient is computed from an estimated adhesion coefficient and the speed, or a fuzzy compensation current that depends on the adhesion coefficient is added to the conventional map [7]. These methods rely on quantities that are not directly measured, and the evidence reported in [2,3,7] comes from simulation. Third, controller-level studies address the stability of the map with a lead–lag compensator [1], the nonlinearity and current saturation of the motor loop [8], and the combination of neural-fuzzy inference with field-oriented control [9]; their target is vibration, saturation and disturbance rejection on a nominal road, not a friction-independent torque target. Fourth, the steering torque itself has been used as the control target: in [5] a reference steering-wheel torque map defined by the steering angle, the steering angular velocity and the vehicle speed, taken from vehicle tests with a highly rated steering feel, is tracked with adaptive sliding-mode control, and the results are given on a dry road (friction coefficient 0.85).

Within these sources, three issues remain open. (i) Friction-aware methods need estimates of friction or axle load, and the maps they select remain open-loop. (ii) The torque-tracking work in [5] defines the reference from the steering angle, angular velocity and speed and evaluates it on a dry road, so the behavior of the reference and of the tracking when friction changes is not addressed, and it does not explain why a tuned assist map cannot remove the deviation by itself. (iii) A comparison of assist maps and torque feedback under common motor and sensor constraints, reporting the trade-off between tracking and assist-torque noise and the occurrence of reverse assistance, is not available. The first two issues have a structural reason. At a column equilibrium the measured torque satisfies T_s = T_r − T_a(T_s); with a local map slope s_m, a change ΔT_r of the reaction torque produces a deviation ΔT_s = ΔT_r/(1 + s_m), which is finite, and s_m is bounded by the stability and noise limits of the loop [1]. An integral term has no such bound at zero frequency.

This study therefore examines a lateral-acceleration-based steering-torque tracking framework for C-EPS under varying road friction. The desired torque is defined from the measured speed and lateral acceleration, and the difference between this reference and the measured column torque is the control error. A conventional assist map with lead–lag compensation is compared with proportional–integral (PI) and proportional–integral–derivative (PID) torque feedback in a nonlinear steering–vehicle model with tire saturation, motor dynamics and noisy sensors.

The main contributions are as follows:
- Defining the torque-tracking error with a reference indexed by vehicle speed and lateral acceleration, so that no friction estimate is required.
- Deriving the local steady-state sensitivity ΔT_s = ΔT_r/(1 + s_m) of an assist map and showing that its slope is limited by loop stability and noise, in contrast with integral feedback.
- Comparing assist-map, PI and PID controllers with common motor and sensor limits, calibration Pareto fronts and independent scenarios, and reporting the tracking–noise trade-off, reverse assistance, and the deviations relative to the driver's discrimination threshold.
