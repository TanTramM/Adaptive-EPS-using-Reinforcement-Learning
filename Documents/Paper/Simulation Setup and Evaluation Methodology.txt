4. Simulation Setup and Evaluation Methodology
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

4.1. Simulation Model and Implementation

The three controllers are evaluated using the nonlinear C-EPS–vehicle model described in Section 2. The adopted steering, vehicle, and tire parameters are summarized in Table 2. Steering-column parameters are based on [1], whereas the tire and vehicle model formulations follow [12,13]. The steering ratio and trail dimensions are prescribed modeling parameters. The magnitude of the friction-induced steering-torque reduction depends on the assumed zero-slip pneumatic trail. For a friction decrease from μ = 0.8 to 0.3 under the fixed-lateral-acceleration condition considered in the sensitivity analysis, varying $t_0$ from 17 to 35 mm changes the predicted reduction in sensed torque from approximately 6% to 13% without motor assistance. The nominal parameter value gives a reduction of about 9–10%. The model therefore captures the expected direction of the effect, while its magnitude remains dependent on the assumed steering geometry.

The model is evaluated between 20 and 100 km/h and friction coefficients from 0.2 to 0.8. Below 20 km/h, the inverse-speed terms in the vehicle equations and the quasi-static tire assumptions become less reliable. The feasible lateral-acceleration range is limited by tire friction and by the small-angle assumptions. At 20 km/h, the adopted model permits approximately 0.2 g, whereas the upper limit approaches the friction-dependent tire-force limit at higher speeds. Calibration and test points are therefore restricted to feasible operating conditions rather than the full rectangular range of the reference table. Equal tire–road friction is assumed on the left and right sides of the vehicle, and the steering-wheel angle is prescribed independently of the driver's response to steering torque.

The motor dynamics are approximated by a first-order transfer function [1]:
(39)  G_m(s) = omega_m/(s + omega_m),   omega_m = 2*pi*(100) rad/s
[ G_m(s)=\dfrac{\omega_m}{s+\omega_m},\quad \omega_m=2\pi(100)\ \mathrm{rad/s} ]
\noindent The common motor model includes a 1 ms computation delay and the speed-dependent assist limit of Eq. (19). The limit is calculated from the maximum dry-road assistance required to reach the reference, plus a margin equal to the Coulomb friction amplitude:
(40)  T_a,max(v) = max over 0 <= a_y <= a_y,lim(v) of [T_r(v, a_y, 0.8) - T_ref(v, a_y)] + T_f
[ T_{a,max}(v)=\max_{0\le a_y\le a_{y,lim}(v)}\left[T_r(v,a_y,0.8)-T_{ref}(v,a_y)\right]+T_f ]
(41)  a_y,lim(v) = min(a_y,max(v, 0.8), 0.4g)
[ a_{y,lim}(v)=\min\left(a_{y,max}(v,0.8),\ 0.4g\right) ]
\noindent where $a_{y,max}(v,0.8)$ denotes the maximum attainable lateral acceleration under the adopted dry-road model. This limit is derived from the simulation model and does not represent a validated capacity of a production EPS motor. It is applied by the same actuator block to all three controllers, so no controller carries its own copy of the limit.

All controllers operate at a sampling period $T_c=1\ \mathrm{ms}$. The lead–lag compensator of Eq. (24) is discretized using the Tustin method, while the PID integrator employs forward Euler integration with back-calculation anti-windup. The integral and filtered derivative states are updated as
(42)  u_I[k+1] = u_I[k] + T_c*(K_i*e_T[k] + K_aw*(T_a,lim[k] - T_a,cmd[k]))
[ u_I[k+1]=u_I[k]+T_c\left(K_ie_T[k]+K_{aw}(T_{a,lim}[k]-T_{a,cmd}[k])\right) ]
(43)  d[k] = a_d*d[k-1] + b_d*(T_s[k] - T_s[k-1])
[ d[k]=a_d\,d[k-1]+b_d\left(T_s[k]-T_s[k-1]\right) ]
(44)  a_d = T_f/(T_f + T_c),   b_d = K_d/(T_f + T_c)
[ a_d=\dfrac{T_f}{T_f+T_c},\quad b_d=\dfrac{K_d}{T_f+T_c} ]
\noindent where $T_{a,lim}$ denotes the assist-torque command after application of the common motor-block saturation limit, and the anti-windup gain $K_{aw}$ is given by Eq. (34) for PID and by Eq. (38) for MapPID. The difference between the limited and unlimited commands provides the back-calculation signal that prevents integrator windup.

Sensor noise, quantization, and update periods are modeled according to Table 3, with resolutions taken from the sensor data sheets [14–16]. The noise standard deviation equals one resolution step for each sensor, except for vehicle speed, for which 0.38 km/h is adopted [17]. The 10 ms vehicle-signal update period is an implementation assumption.

Table 3. Sensor and actuator settings.
Signal or component | Resolution or setting | Update period

Steering-wheel angle $\theta_1$ | 0.1° | 1 ms

The motor current loop and electrical power constraints are not modeled. The initial 2 s of each simulation are excluded from performance evaluation. Because the torque sensor resolves 0.01 N·m and the lateral-acceleration quantization step of 0.01 g moves the reference by roughly 0.03–0.05 N·m, torque errors are rounded to 0.01 N·m in this paper, and a steady-state error or a difference between controllers below this level is described as indistinguishable from zero rather than quoted as a number.

4.2. Controller Calibration and Selection

The controllers are calibrated on a single 140 s scenario, CALIB, which is separate from the evaluation scenarios of Section 4.3. The vehicle speed increases from 20 to 100 km/h in nine blocks of 10 km/h on a dry road (μ = 0.8). The steering-wheel angle is prescribed open loop from the steady-state angle table, so the lateral acceleration is a label for the commanded angle and is not visible to the controllers. Each block contains a staircase of held turns at 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 g (0.10 and 0.18 g at 20 km/h, where the model reaches only about 0.2 g), each level lasting 3 s: a 1 s ramp, 1 s of settling, and a 1 s evaluation window. From 40 km/h upward, the staircase is followed by sinusoidal steering through zero at 0.5 Hz (1.25 cycles, 0.3 g amplitude) and at 1 Hz (3.5 cycles, 0.15 g amplitude), which exposes the dead zone around the straight-ahead position and the sign change of the assistance. Sinusoidal steering is omitted below 40 km/h, where the open-loop driver would need 80–144° of steering-wheel angle and the column inertia dominates the sensed torque for every controller. At 60 km/h, the friction coefficient falls from 0.8 to 0.35 during the 0.3 g level and returns to 0.8 after 6 s. The scenario defines 68 evaluation windows: 26 steady-state, 26 transient, 14 sinusoidal, and 2 friction-transition windows.

Tracking accuracy and high-frequency assist-torque content are quantified by
(45)  R_g = 100*RMS_g(e_T)/mean_g(|T_ref|),   R = (R_static * R_dyn * R_mu)^(1/3)
[ R_g=100\dfrac{\mathrm{RMS}_g(e_T)}{\mathrm{mean}_g(|T_{ref}|)},\quad R=\left(R_{static}R_{dyn}R_{\mu}\right)^{1/3} ]
(46)  S = RMS(T_a - F_5(T_a))
[ S=\mathrm{RMS}\left(T_a-F_5(T_a)\right) ]
\noindent where $g$ denotes one of three window groups: steady-state (static), transient and sinusoidal (dyn), and friction-transition (mu). Each $R_g$ is bounded below by 1%, which is two to three times the torque-sensor step, so that errors below the sensor resolution are not rewarded. The geometric mean prevents the large dynamic group from dominating the small steady-state group. The metric S is the RMS difference between the applied assist torque and its first-order low-pass-filtered counterpart with a 5 Hz cutoff $F_5(\cdot)$. It quantifies the assist-torque content that the steering command, whose band is below about 2 Hz [11], cannot explain, which arises mainly from sensor-noise amplification. For the steady-state windows, the signed relative error is
(47)  e_rel = 100*sgn(T_ref)*mean(e_T)/mean(|T_ref|)
[ e_{rel}=100\dfrac{\mathrm{sgn}(T_{ref})\,\mathrm{mean}(e_T)}{\mathrm{mean}(|T_{ref}|)} ]
\noindent RMS error is used for the transient, sinusoidal, and friction-transition windows, where a signed relative error may be misleading.

A candidate design must satisfy three admissibility requirements before it is ranked. First, the maximum of $|e_{rel}|$ over the 26 steady-state windows must not exceed 10%, which is the smallest just-noticeable difference of steering-wheel torque measured for drivers [18]. Second, the stability margin is checked by simulation: the calibration is repeated with the motor gain doubled and, separately, with an extra 1 ms delay between the controller and the motor, and R in both cases must remain finite and no greater than twice its nominal value. These are specified perturbation tests rather than a general guarantee of robust stability; the delay of one control period is a design choice that covers unmodeled computation, communication, and current-loop lag [19]. Third, to preserve the 0.2–2 Hz steering-response band [11], the lag stage of the assist-map compensator and the first-order derivative filter of PID are constrained to a magnitude response no lower than −3 dB at 2 Hz. A linear screening step discards designs whose phase margin is below 30° or gain margin below 2 on the linearized models.

The design variables are searched on grids that are refined in two stages around the best feasible region. The assist map has two variables (the slope limit $K_{max}$ and the lowest lag gain over 0.2–2 Hz). PID has four ($K_p$, $K_i$, $K_d$, $T_f$). In MapPID, the map branch is fixed to the Map design and the four PID variables are searched, so that any change relative to Map is attributable to the feedback branch. Every candidate is simulated on CALIB with the noise realization that is local to its controller, and the feasible candidates form a Pareto front in the (R, S) plane. The comparison between controllers is made on the whole front; the front points of all three controllers are re-simulated on five common noise realizations (seeds 99990 to 99994), because S depends on the noise sequence. The single operating point used in Section 5 is the admissible design with the smallest product
(48)  J = R*S
[ J=R\cdot S ]
\noindent which trades a percentage of tracking accuracy against the same percentage of assist-torque noise and is independent of the units of R and S.

4.3. Independent Evaluation Scenarios

After calibration, each controller is tested on five independent 60 s scenarios whose conditions lie outside the CALIB grid of speeds, lateral accelerations, friction coefficients, and steering frequencies. The scenarios were frozen before any controller was run on them, and the steering input is again the prescribed open-loop wheel angle. The conditions and principal evaluation measures are summarized in Table 4.

Table 4. Calibration and independent evaluation scenarios.
Scenario | Duration | Main operating conditions | Question addressed
CALIB | 140 s | 20–100 km/h; held turns at 0.1–0.3 g; sinusoidal steering at 0.5 and 1 Hz; μ = 0.8 → 0.35 → 0.8 at 60 km/h | Controller selection
DRY | 60 s | Held turns of 0.15 g (left) and 0.25 g (right) at 25, 45, 75, and 95 km/h; μ = 0.8 | Steady-state error at speeds and levels not used for calibration
RAMP | 60 s | 0.2 g held turn while speed falls from 90 to 40 km/h and rises back; μ = 0.8 | Tracking while the reference changes continuously with speed
SLIP | 60 s | Friction step 0.8 → 0.5 at 70 km/h, 0.2 g; 1 s puddle of μ = 0.2 at 85 km/h, 0.15 g; step 0.4 → 0.8 at 55 km/h, 0.2 g | Light steering after a friction fall, heavy steering and recovery after a rise
EDGE | 60 s | Held turns of 0.35–0.38 g at 50 and 80 km/h; fast entry to 0.35 g at 65 km/h; μ = 0.8 | Behavior near the assist limit, outside the design range
WEAVE | 60 s | Steering near center (0.05 g, 0.2 Hz, 100 km/h); double lane change (0.25 g, 0.6 Hz, 80 km/h); slalom on μ = 0.3 (0.15 g, 0.75 Hz, 60 km/h) | Noise at center, phase lag in fast steering, continuous steering

\noindent The lateral-acceleration values in Table 4 label the prescribed wheel angle. At 0.6 and 0.75 Hz, the lateral acceleration reached by the vehicle is about 12% below the labeled amplitude because the open-loop driver uses the steady-state angle table; this applies equally to all controllers. The load on the tire, |a_y|/μ, does not exceed 0.75 in any scenario that contains a friction step.

Each scenario is run with five noise realizations (seeds 99995 to 99999), identical for all controllers. Results are reported as the mean and the sample standard deviation over the five realizations, and two values that differ by less than 5% or by less than the standard deviation across realizations are treated as equal. The reported measures are the signed steady-state relative error of Eq. (47) in each hold window, the RMS and maximum of $e_T$, the high-frequency content S, the total variation of $T_a$, the fraction of time in which the steering feels heavy ($|T_s|>1.1|T_{ref}|$) or light ($|T_s|<0.9|T_{ref}|$), the fraction of time at 98% of $T_{a,max}$, and the time to recover to $|e_T|<10\%|T_{ref}|$ after a friction rise. Reverse assistance is the fraction of evaluated samples for which $T_aT_s$ is negative and $|T_s|$ is at least 0.5 N·m. No measure is combined into a weighted total. The minimum assistance available to the driver is bounded by $|T_a|\le T_{a,max}$ in all three controllers, and no separate heavy-steering test with the assistance removed is performed.
